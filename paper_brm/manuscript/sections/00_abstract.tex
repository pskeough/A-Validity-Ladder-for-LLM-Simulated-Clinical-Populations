A simulated sample built by prompting a language model with demographic personas can be used to report a persona's score, to stand in for individual respondents, to compare groups, to estimate a population's level or to score a multi-item scale.
Each use assumes something different about how well the model replicates people, and validation practice in the field remains uneven.
We propose the validity ladder, a gate and four levels released as code, in which every rung tests one of these uses against human data with a statistic and a pass rule, and a sample supports only the claims whose rungs it passes.
In a worked example of 28,800 PHQ-8 depression assessments from four models, compared against a national health survey, a few repeated draws make each persona's mean readable.
Above the gate every model fails, with answers less varied than people's, an exaggerated income gradient, every group placed too high and a distorted scale structure.
Real survey respondents put through the same design are rarely failed, while failures planted into them are caught at the level each targets.
Tested on three published silicon samples, the ladder supports only a few partisan gaps, pointing toward narrower readings of those samples' claims.
These results show that each claim drawn from a simulated sample needs its own check against the human data it claims to represent.
