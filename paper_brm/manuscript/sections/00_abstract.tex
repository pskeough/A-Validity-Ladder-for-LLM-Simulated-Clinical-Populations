Behavioral researchers prompt large language models with demographic personas and use the answers in place of survey respondents, and the usual check on such a sample is a reading of the answers.
No ordered set of tests, each with a pass rule and a population reference, exists for a researcher to run on one model and report.
The validity ladder, released as code, is a gate and four levels.
The gate measures how many repeated draws make a persona's score precise enough to read.
The four levels test single answers, gaps between groups, the population's level and the instrument's structure against human data.
Each level carries a statistic, a pass rule and the use a pass supports, so a sample supports only the claims whose levels it passes.
In a worked example of 28,800 PHQ-8 assessments from four models, read against the NHANES national health survey, persona means are readable after a few draws and every model fails all four levels.
The models give less varied answers than people, exaggerate the income gradient, sit above the survey's level and lose the scale's structure.
Survey respondents run through the same design pass the gate and levels 1 and 3 and are rarely failed elsewhere, and planted failures trip the level each targets.
On three published silicon samples, partisan gaps hold where the data can test them, and most demographic gaps fail or cannot be read.
Each claim drawn from a simulated sample needs its own check against human data.
