\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In the worked example and the three external datasets, every sample failed at least one check on a use that researchers make of such samples.
Every finding drawn from a simulated sample therefore needs its own check.
A group gap, a population level and a scale score are separate extrapolations, and a pass on one carries no information about the others.
Researchers running persona studies should hold each extrapolation in critical doubt, audit it against the level that tests it, and treat a claim without that check as unsupported.
The ladder makes the audit routine, and the controls show that it passes real respondents and detects failures of a known size.

Every level above the gate rests on human data.
A national survey or a private validated dataset on the same instrument has to be in hand before the simulated sample is generated, because the reference fixes which personas can be compared, how many draws they need and which gaps the survey can measure at all.
The cleanest reference comes from personas built on real respondents' profiles, as in the releases of \citet{bisbee2024synthetic} and \citet{argyle2023outofone}, where every simulated answer has a human twin.

For published silicon samples, the ladder narrows claims more often than it overturns them.
Partisan gaps in the releases of \citet{bisbee2024synthetic} and \citet{argyle2023outofone} hold, while most gaps by sex, age and race fail or cannot be read, so claims of fidelity from these samples apply within partisan comparisons.
The ladder leaves the substantive question to the human data.
Its contribution is a stronger context for each claim and a screen against readings of a persona sample that the data do not support.

The worked example covers four base models under one prompt family, with one wording for each attribute, one persona per demographic cell, no age in the personas and the PHQ-8 items in a fixed order.
Transgender and multiracial personas have no comparison group in NHANES and are not tested at levels 2 and 3.
The corpus serves as a baseline against which grounded or fine-tuned simulators can be tested with the same ladder, and the code for every rung is released for that purpose.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

The validity ladder turns the question of whether a simulated sample can stand in for people into a set of checks, one for each use, each with a pass rule and a population reference.
Applied to 28,800 PHQ-8 assessments from four models, it reads every model's persona means and fails every model on every use above them. % R: corpus_v3_provenance.csv; l1_personfit_main.csv
Applied to three published silicon samples, it separates the uses each release supports from those it does not.
